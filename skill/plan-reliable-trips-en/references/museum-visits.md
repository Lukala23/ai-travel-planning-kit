# Museums, Exhibitions, Memorials, and Interpretation

Read when the user names a venue, a museum is selected for the route, or a light destination scan shows that it is central to the place's value. Capitals, famous cities, and historic/cultural destinations increase the chance of a scan but do not automatically trigger this full module. Once active, treat museums as potential core experiences rather than a ticket-and-hours appendix, while rejecting fame without real visitor value.

## 1. Candidate discovery and value

Evaluate what a visitor can actually see during the trip:

- uniqueness, representative objects/spaces, exhibition quality, current galleries, and relation to the place;
- how much of a famous collection is publicly displayed and whether key rooms are closed, moved, or on loan;
- fit with the user's interests, duplication with other venues, fatigue, language access, time, ticket, queue, and route cost;
- whether a smaller or less marketed venue offers stronger context, intact space, specialist collection, architecture, or local history.

Popularity, collection size, national title, or social-media visibility does not by itself prove value. Conversely, do not omit a venue merely because it is small or not globally famous.

Use `Core / Strong optional / Replaceable / Exclude` and state the actual reason. A venue may become a route anchor when its value, timed entry, closure pattern, or visit duration determines the day.

## 2. Current-state verification

Verify the exact branch/building, open galleries, temporary exhibition, closure day, timed-entry rules, security, bag policy, last entry, accessibility, photography, interpretation, and on-site services for the actual date.

Distinguish:

- permanent collection access from a temporary-exhibition ticket;
- free entry from reservation, donation, or paid special exhibition;
- official ticket from reseller, tour, membership, city pass, or bundled transport;
- general opening from room-level closure, strike, renovation, or capacity restriction.

Use recent visitor evidence to supplement wayfinding, queues, fatigue, and confusing layouts, but use the venue or responsible authority for current operation.

## 3. Preferred-language access

Determine the travelers' preferred interpretation language from the current trip. Do not infer it from the language of this Skill, passport, residence, or destination; ask only when language access changes the recommendation.

Check:

- official human tours and their language, schedule, group size, route, booking, and cancellation;
- official audio guide, app, device, QR/web guide, printed guide, captions, and accessibility media;
- qualified private guide or licensed tour, meeting point, duration, scope, credentials, and whether ticket is included;
- pre-reading plus self-guided route when no good language service exists.

Do not write “audio guide available” without confirming the exact language and whether it covers the relevant galleries. Do not assume an unofficial guide may operate inside the venue.

A “skip-the-line” claim must identify what queue is skipped. It may mean only ticket purchase, not security, identity check, timed-entry, immigration, or capacity control.

## 5. Internal visit route

For every core or time-intensive venue, provide:

- an orientation point and the correct entrance/building;
- `Highlights route`: essential rooms/objects and a realistic short duration;
- `Deep route`: additional rooms/themes and realistic total time;
- representative objects or spaces, why they matter, and what a non-specialist should notice;
- floor/wing/order logic, stairs/lifts, cloakroom, toilets, seating, food, re-entry, and exit/shop only when useful;
- a shortening rule for fatigue, late arrival, closure, or an overcrowded gallery.

Do not produce a giant object list. Select a coherent narrative and acknowledge when famous objects may be absent. Include queue, security, cloakroom, orientation, rest, and exit in the total time rather than quoting gallery time alone.

## 6. Route integration

- Coordinate timed entry, opening/closing, weekday patterns, nearby meal, accommodation, evening walk, and onward transport.
- Avoid stacking multiple exhausting museums merely because they are geographically close.
- Use a museum intelligently on bad-weather days, but do not promise last-minute capacity.
- For major museum cities, compare overlaps and distribute themes; for smaller destinations, include a truly valuable local venue even if it is not the city's headline attraction.
- Put relevant local food, book/design shop, museum derivative, or nearby historic context into the same route only when it improves the day.

## 7. Photography, ethics, and souvenirs

Check flash, tripod, video, portrait, security, sacred/human remains, memorial, copyright, and commercial-use rules. Provide a concise composition tip only for architecture, displays, or public spaces where photography is allowed and meaningful.

Museum shops may be good for authoritative reproductions, books, design objects, and compact gifts, but compare price and distinctiveness with local makers, bookshops, markets, and other channels. Do not turn the exit shop into a required stop.

## 8. Multi-venue comparison

When several venues compete for limited time, use:
